ABSTRACT
What makes great scientists great? Even as AI systems start to make progress on
open problems, scientists remain far ahead of them at sensing which prior idea,
buried in an ever-growing archive of research, a new problem needs. To study this
skill, we draw on researchers who know firsthand which earlier work advanced
their completed projects, with papers serving as pointers to the ideas within. Using
our automated pipeline that makes author annotation scalable, we build SCHOL-
ARCATALYST by having 184 lead authors of 207 recent computer science papers
label which candidates did or could have advanced their project, each with a de-
tailed rationale. We introduce a retrieval task with author-provided judgments:
given an initial research question, retrieve these papers from only the literature
available when the project began. Agentic search does no better than embedding
retrieval (0.42 vs. 0.48 Recall@20) despite calling that same retriever as a tool.
Even an agent built on Claude Fable 5.1, which may have seen the completed pa-
pers during training, reaches only 0.51 R@20. These results highlight the need for
new training recipes that equip models with expert intuition for searching broad
corpora. We envision SCHOLARCATALYST as a step toward scientific agents that
can take a half-formed idea and point to the prior research it needs.

INTRODUCTION
To invent is to discern, to choose.— Henri Poincaré
Scientific research is inherently cumulative, with each project building on previous results and ideas.
Current AI systems for research mainly accelerate progress on an already well-defined subproblem,
where the remaining work is closer to engineering. Deciding which existing idea a new problem
should build on is still mostly left to human researchers. An insightful prior paper can turn a vague
question into a well-defined problem, for instance by revealing a limitation that motivates a new di-
rection or suggesting an approach worth adapting, thereby shaping and accelerating—catalyzing—
subsequent research. Making this choice well is a defining ability of great scientists, and a critical
part of what researchers call research taste. We ask whether models can make that decision through
a concrete task: given an early-stage research question, find its catalyst papers, by which we mean
prior papers whose ideas did or could have meaningfully advanced the project (Figure 1).
Evaluating this retrieval task requires ground truth about which papers were catalysts for a particular
project. Published papers record the outcomes of completed projects, but the ideation that shaped
them is largely undocumented, and bibliographies rarely distinguish the sources that provided non-
obvious, critical insights from the broader set of cited works. Existing scientific retrieval benchmarks
(Table 1) evaluate facet-level similarity or relevance to a stated information need (Wadden et al.,
2020; Wang et al., 2023; Ajith et al., 2024; Asai et al., 2024) or derive positives from citation-intent
annotations for queries that withhold the eventual method (Garikaparthi et al., 2025). These signals
do not capture whether a retrieved paper could have inspired an early-stage project, especially when
it was not cited by the eventual paper.
We introduce SCHOLARCATALYST, a literature inspiration retrieval benchmark grounded in re-
searchers’ firsthand knowledge of their own projects. 184 researchers who led 207 recent computer
science projects verified 894 research questions as they stood before each project’s key findings,
then labeled which prior papers did or could have advanced their work and explained why, including
papers they had not encountered at the time. Given a question, a system must rank these papers near
the top of a 191K-paper corpus restricted to work published before the project, using models whose
knowledge cutoffs predate every source paper. This knowledge is rarely documented, and we collect
it efficiently and at scale through an automated pipeline that only needs a paper’s arXiv ID to pro-
duce author-reviewable draft data. Because any newly published paper can become a fresh instance,
the pipeline keeps SCHOLARCATALYST ahead of model training cutoffs and lets the benchmark
grow with the literature.
Across sparse, dense, and multi-vector retrievers and LLM search agents, the strongest system re-
covers only 48% of gold (i.e., author-credited) papers in its top 20. We find that topical similarity
is a weak signal: catalyst papers are no more similar to the query than related papers the authors
rejected. Every search an agent issues still goes through a similarity-based retriever, so repeated
querying does not help (42% vs. 48% for the retriever alone). We also evaluate Claude Fable 5.1
as a lenient upper reference because its training data may include the source papers or secondary
sources describing them, and even with this advantage it misses nearly half of the gold papers in its
top 20. More broadly, our results point to the need for retrieval models with an expert-level sense of
which ideas matter, and SCHOLARCATALYST offers a way to measure progress toward them.

RELATED WORK
Scientific discovery using LLMs.
Accelerating science is a major opportunity for LLM-based
agents, which increasingly can propose relevant hypotheses and research ideas (Wang et al., 2024a;b;
Liu et al., 2025; 2026). We deliberately ground our task in existing papers. Unlike a newly generated
idea, which cannot be verified without running it (Si et al., 2025; 2026), a published paper comes
with a record of what worked, why, and where it fell short. The same grounding is what lets us collect
ground truth, since authors can judge existing papers against what actually advanced their project.
Existing scientific-discovery systems likewise draw on prior papers, either starting from a seed or
target paper and exploiting its citation graph or metadata (Wang et al., 2024a; Liu et al., 2025),
while others search a task-specific candidate corpus constructed in advance (Wang et al., 2024b;
Liu et al., 2026). Recent work studies how LLMs can generate scientific insights by combining
existing ideas (He-Yueya et al., 2026), further motivating the need to evaluate which prior work
agents choose to draw on. SCHOLARCATALYST directly addresses this evaluation gap by testing
whether scientific agents can retrieve insightful prior ideas for an early-stage research question,
guided by project authors’ firsthand experience of what helped their research.
Scientific literature retrieval.
Existing scientific retrieval benchmarks evaluate how well systems
find evidence for claims (Wadden et al., 2020; 2022), retrieve documents for specified facets (Mysore
et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023), and locate papers given document context or explicit information
needs (Ajith et al., 2024; Asai et al., 2024; He et al., 2025; Park et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2025).
Whereas existing benchmarks provide a retrieval objective that is explicit in the query, we pose
an open-ended problem that requires inferring the latent scientific relevance of papers to an early-
stage research idea. MIR (Garikaparthi et al., 2025) comes closest, since its queries also withhold
the project’s eventual method, but it derives positives from citation-intent annotations indicating
methodological influence (Lauscher et al., 2022). Rather than inferring relevance from the pub-
lished record, we elicit directly from paper authors otherwise undocumented data about the research
process behind it, including early-stage research questions and relevance judgments. To our knowl-
edge, SCHOLARCATALYST is the first scientific literature retrieval benchmark grounded in authors’
firsthand accounts of their research process.
Information retrieval systems.
Lexical retrievers (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009) and embedding
retrievers rank corpus papers through term overlap or learned semantic similarity (Karpukhin et al.,
2020; Khattab & Zaharia, 2020; Zhang et al., 2025b; Shanbhogue et al., 2026). For a given query,
these methods rank the corpus once and do not adapt the search to the returned papers. Search
agents use LLM reasoning to iteratively formulate queries and invoke these retrievers as tools over
multiple turns, enabling more adaptive search for complex information-seeking tasks (Yao et al.,
2022; Jin et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). Deep research systems extend this approach with subquestion
decomposition, reading, and reflection over longer horizons (Team et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a).
However, these agents still access the corpus through a retriever’s top-k candidates at each step.
Recent work instead gives agents direct access to the raw corpus through terminal tools such as
grep and file reads, letting the language model determine how to search and interpret documents (Li
et al., 2026; Sen et al., 2026). Whether such agentic search approaches can retrieve prior work whose
scientific inspiration is latent rather than lexically explicit remains unclear.

SCHOLARCATALYST: EVALUATING SCIENTIFIC LITERATURE RETRIEVAL
WITH AUTHORS’ FIRSTHAND ACCOUNTS
SCHOLARCATALYST evaluates whether retrieval systems can identify catalyst papers, prior work
whose ideas could inspire and help advance an early stage of research. To obtain firsthand judg-
ments about inspiration, we ask project authors which prior papers did or could have helped advance
their project and why, knowledge that is largely absent from the public record. We first define the
task of retrieving useful prior work, including its query types, retrieval corpus, and evaluation met-
rics (Section 3.1). We then describe our pipeline that supports effective and efficient data collection
(Section 3.2) and the subsequent author validation and revision process for finalizing the benchmark
instances (Section 3.3).

3.1
TASK DEFINITION
Given a query q from the authors of the source paper S, the task is to retrieve catalyst papers D+
q from
P<S, where P<S is a corpus containing papers published before source paper S was completed. For
each query q, we also provide hard-negative documents, D−
q ⊂P<S, which are related to q but did
not inspire the authors of S.
Each query q is a short paragraph that reconstructs the research landscape at the outset of the project
underlying the source paper S. It describes what was known, what remained unresolved, and the
open question the authors set out to answer, without revealing the eventual solution. Specifically,
we consider two query types that reflect how an early-stage project develops from a broad central
research question into particular subfields, with an inspiring paper potentially shaping the next step
at either stage.
• A core research query (CoreQ) asks which prior ideas could help address the project’s central
research question.
• A subfield-specific query (SubQ) approaches the same research question from a particular re-
search subfield, asking which prior ideas could help advance the project from that perspective.

By making the retrieval corpus P<S contain only papers published before S (Yetisgen-Yildiz &
Pratt, 2008; 2009; Halawi et al., 2024), we approximate the literature available during the research
process. We also disable external web access to prevent retrieval of subsequent writing about the
project and the source paper. We evaluate retrieval performance using Recall@N, which measures
the fraction of positive papers retrieved in the top N results, and normalized discounted cumulative
gain (nDCG@N) (Järvelin & Kekäläinen, 2002), which additionally rewards higher rankings of
positive papers.

3.2
OUR AUTOMATED PIPELINE FOR EFFICIENT DATA COLLECTION
We build an automated data construction pipeline that only requires a source paper’s arXiv ID and
produces the materials required for author annotation. The pipeline let us collect author-validated
data at scale. It also makes it easy to refresh SCHOLARCATALYST with newly published papers as
older instances go stale due to leakage into newer models’ training data.
Source-paper and corpus processing. For each source paper S, we use the arXiv API1 to obtain
metadata and source files, parse the full text into a structured representation, and resolve bibli-
ography entries to canonical scholarly records. The resulting one-hop citation graph links inline
citations to the referenced papers’ metadata and abstracts. Our retrieval corpus P contains 190,896
papers. It combines references resolved from our source papers with 181K additional arXiv papers
from 2020–2024 across major computer-science categories. Scoping P to computer science and the
source papers’ publication years keeps the corpus relevant to SCHOLARCATALYST without indexing
all of arXiv, which now exceeds 3 million articles (arXiv, 2026). We show detailed corpus statistics
in Section B.2.
Pre-annotation and candidate construction. We use Gemini 3.1 Pro to generate pre-annotations
from the structured source paper S and its bibliography. These include one CoreQ, several SubQs,
and rationales explaining the proposed relevance of cited papers. We additionally retrieve uncited
candidates with BM25 and Qwen3-Embedding-8B (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009; Zhang et al.,
2025b). Gemini 3.6 Flash (Google DeepMind, 2026b) reranks the pooled candidates for scientific
relevance, and the pipeline retains 10 candidates per query to balance coverage of potential false
negatives with a manageable author-review workload.
The resulting pre-annotations and candidates are loaded into a web interface for author review (Sec-
tion 3.3). We describe the implementation details of our pipeline in Section E.

EVALUATION AND ANALYSIS
Our evaluation corpus of 191K computer-science papers is far smaller than the three million papers
on arXiv (arXiv, 2026), among which researchers are able to find work that inspires a project. Re-
trieval in this corpus is therefore an easier version of the problem researchers actually face. In this
section, we ask: can current retrieval systems identify the catalyst papers that inspired a project
from among the many topically related papers in the corpus? If not, where do they fail?
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
We evaluate lexical, multi-vector, dense, and agentic retrieval.
We include BM25 (Robertson
& Zaragoza, 2009) as a lexical baseline, since it performs strongly against dense models on
BEIR (Thakur et al., 2021) and LongEmbed (Zhu et al., 2024), and LateOn (Sourty et al., 2026) as
a multi-vector baseline. We evaluate seven dense retrievers. Qwen3-Embedding-4B and 8B (Zhang
et al., 2025b) and Gemini Embedding 2 (Shanbhogue et al., 2026) are general-purpose models.
SPECTER2 (Singh et al., 2022) and OpenScholar (Asai et al., 2024) are trained on scientific docu-
ments. ReasonEmbed (Chen et al., 2025) and Inf-Retriever-v1-Pro (Yao et al., 2025) are optimized
for reasoning-intensive retrieval. Appendix D.1 provides extended results for further embedding
retrievers.
We evaluate three search agents that represent common designs in the literature. The grep agent
follows DCI-Agent-Lite (Li et al., 2026) and searches titles and abstracts with regular expressions.
The tool-calling agent follows the search-then-select structure for literature search (He et al., 2025;
Pan et al., 2026). The deep research agent adopts the workflow of deep research systems (OpenAI,
2025; Zhang et al., 2025a): it breaks the query into subquestions and iterates over search, reading,
and synthesis. The latter two agents retrieve candidates for their search queries with Gemini Em-
bedding 2. We use GPT-4.1 (OpenAI, 2026a) and o3 (OpenAI, 2026e) as backbone models since
they are among the strongest models with a knowledge cutoff (June 2024) before all source pa-
pers. To compare against a recent frontier model, we also evaluate Claude Fable 5.1 (Anthropic,
2026), which may have seen most source papers during training (June 2026 cutoff). Following pre-
vious work (Thakur et al., 2021; Su et al., 2025; Tchuindjo et al., 2026), we report nDCG@20 and
Recall@k (k = 5, 20, 100). Full experimental details are provided in Section C.

4.2
MAIN RESULTS
All systems struggle to retrieve catalyst papers. Current retrievers miss most of the papers that
inspired a project (Table 3). General-purpose dense retrievers perform best, yet place 39% of author-
identified inspiration papers in the top 20 for CoreQ and 51% for SubQ. Scientific-document retriev-
ers (SPECTER2, OpenScholar) trail the general-purpose models by at least 16 points on CoreQ and
27 points on SubQ at Recall@20. BM25 and LateOn trail by 15–16 points on CoreQ and 18–25
points on SubQ. Together, these results suggest that domain-specific training on citation signals
does not by itself solve this task. Among the pre-cutoff systems in our main experiment, CoreQ
is harder than SubQ, consistent with its longer queries and smaller, almost entirely cited positive
sets (Table 2). We show later that reranking with an oracle candidate pool recovers part of this gap
(Section 4.4).
Agentic search does not improve over embedding retrieval. We compare LLM search agents
built on pre-cutoff models (GPT-4.1 and o3) against the strongest embedding model (Table 3). Grep-
based search, despite its recent success as a lightweight alternative to embedding retrieval (Li et al.,
2026; Sen et al., 2026), performs substantially worse. It finds only 8% of gold query–paper pairs
during search, compared with 46% for the tool-calling agent (Figure 4).
We attribute this gap to candidate coverage, since regardless of how a model reasons, an agent
can interact with the corpus only through its search queries. As the corpus scales, this constraint
becomes more important because an agent can inspect only a small fraction of the corpus. Consistent
with this account, agent recall rises with backbone capability yet remains close to the embedding
retriever’s even for the strongest model (Figure 5). If coverage is the limiting factor, progress will
require embedding retrievers that surface inspiration papers in the first place and agents that build
on those results to guide further exploration. We report Fable 5.1 separately because it falls outside

4.3
ANALYSIS OF AUTHOR-ANNOTATED CATALYST PAPERS
Inspiration relations are diverse and not captured by similarity. Prompting an LLM judge to
classify each of the 663 key-inspiration rationales under a nine-type taxonomy (Appendix B.3),
we find that authors most often credit a technique they adapted (method), empirical findings that
supported a direction (evidence), an idea extended to a new setting (generalization), or a limitation
that motivated a new approach (limitation), with more than one type applying to 55.8% of rationales
(Figure 7). Similarity to the query does not identify these papers: measuring lexical similarity as the
fraction of unique query words in a candidate’s title and abstract, and semantic similarity as cosine
similarity under Qwen3-Embedding-8B (Zhang et al., 2025b), hard negatives are at least as similar
to the query as positives on both measures (Table 11). This also limits agentic search, where grep
and embedding tools construct the candidate pool before an agent can reason about which papers
could inspire a project.
The published record does not reveal which papers inspired a project. Source-paper citations
provide an incomplete signal of inspiration. 43.6% of SubQ positives are not cited by the source
paper, and 67.8% of those share no references with it. Among cited papers, citation-intent labels
do not identify inspirations: only 46.2% of CoreQ and 34.0% of SubQ positives carry a uses or
extends label (Lauscher et al., 2022). Even given the entire finished source paper in addition to
its bibliography, LLMs fail to identify catalyst papers. Gemini 3.1 Pro (Google DeepMind, 2026a)
proposes candidate inspiration papers that cover only 32.2% of CoreQ positives on average and none
of the key inspirations in 28.0% of instances. Section B.3 and Table 12 give further details, including
an instance with both uncited positives and cited hard negatives.
4.4
ANALYSIS OF FACTORS BEHIND RETRIEVAL PERFORMANCE
Stronger rerankers benefit more from better candidate coverage. To separate retrieval cover-
age from recognition performance, we apply LLM-based reranking to the top-K candidates from
Gemini-Embedding-2 on a randomly sampled subset of 50 CoreQ and 50 SubQ queries. We com-
8

pare reranking with and without gold injection, which replaces negative candidates with positive
papers missing from the set while keeping the pool size fixed. Stronger rerankers gain more in Re-
call@5 from gold injection (Figure 6). The growing gap suggests that candidate coverage limits how
much we can benefit from stronger rerankers: their ability to select useful papers matters only when
those papers enter the pool. Better retrieval could therefore yield larger gains as rerankers improve.
Reranking prompts and procedures are provided in Section D.2.
Agents benefit from source context but not from fuller reading. We vary two kinds of agent
context: information about the source paper and how much of each retrieved paper the agent reads.
With a Gemini 3.7 Flash (Google DeepMind, 2026c) (cutoff March 2026) tool-calling agent on the
203-query cutoff split (Table 4), the source title and abstract raise Recall@20 by 0.11 for CoreQ
and 0.02 for SubQ, while web search changes it by at most 0.01. The source bibliography, which
contains 95% of CoreQ gold papers, raises it by 0.35. Without search tools, the model given the
title and abstract reaches only 0.31 CoreQ Recall@20, below corpus search alone, so source context
helps by guiding search rather than through memory. In contrast, letting a GPT-4.1 tool-calling
agent read beyond abstracts on 50 queries per type changes Recall@20 by at most 0.04 (Figure 11),
and the best SubQ policy still trails the retriever (0.50 versus 0.55). Together, these results suggest
that the bottleneck lies in knowing what to search for rather than in understanding the papers already
found, consistent with the candidate-coverage explanation above. Source information is used only
in this analysis, and Sections C.3 and D.5 give details.
Query rewriting does not consistently improve retrieval. We test whether an LLM can improve
retrieval by reasoning over the original research question to generate single-query expansions, mul-
tiple queries, or hypothetical abstracts (Gao et al., 2023). We evaluate these strategies on 50 queries
of each type, using GPT-4.1 for generation and Gemini-Embedding-2 for retrieval. Neither single-
query expansion nor multi-query generation yields a meaningful improvement, while both HyDE
variants substantially reduce recall (Table 5). If the retriever’s misses were a matter of phrasing, an
LLM that reads the research question should be able to recover some of them by rewording. That
none of these strategies does so suggests the embedding space itself does not place inspiration pa-
pers near their queries, consistent with the similarity analysis in Section 4.3. We show prompts and
implementation details in Section D.4.

CONCLUSION
We introduced SCHOLARCATALYST, a benchmark for one ingredient of research ability: identifying
prior papers whose ideas could help a project move forward. Drawing on 184 researchers’ own
accounts of what shaped their work, we capture judgments that the published record largely omits.
Current systems recover only a fraction of the papers that authors credit, and agentic search does
no better than embedding retrieval, largely because limited candidate coverage keeps many useful
papers out of reach.
Limitations. SCHOLARCATALYST covers computer science papers from 2025–2026 with uneven
representation across research areas (Table 8), so our findings may not transfer to other fields. Our
labels are retrospective judgments by paper authors and are thus subject to hindsight. A more fun-
damental open question is the skyline, i.e., the performance ceiling of this task. As a point of
reference, the human reference accuracy on ImageNet (94.9% top-5 (Russakovsky et al., 2015)) was
later exceeded, and reannotation studies found that subsequent gains increasingly reflected the id-
iosyncrasies of the original labels (Beyer et al., 2020; Shankar et al., 2020). Catalyst paper retrieval
is harder to audit this way, since relabeling by anyone other than the authors would be costly and
would measure a different target, namely what an informed outsider can infer. Still, a preliminary
comparison on one paper with three author-annotators in SCHOLARCATALYST found that coau-
thors marking only about seven positives per thread recovered 43–60% of the first author’s labels
at 84–88% precision. This is well above the best system’s overall R@5 of 0.24, which suggests
considerable room for improvement.
Outlook. Recent work on reasoning-intensive retrieval evaluates relevance relations that require
reasoning to recognize (Xiao et al., 2024; Su et al., 2025; Tchuindjo et al., 2026). SCHOLARCATA-
LYST pushes further, requiring judgments of how prior ideas could advance a research project. We
see two milestones ahead. The first is expert-level retrieval, matching a domain expert’s judgment
within their own field. The second is retrieval beyond any individual expert: being able to exercise
9

that level of intuition across a breadth of fields no single researcher can follow. This breadth may
be where such systems help most, since nearly half of the papers our authors credit (49.5%) come
from outside their project’s subject area, and 43.6% of subfield-specific positives were never cited
in the source paper. Researchers may therefore be limited more by what they have seen than by how
well they judge it. The full literature makes this gap far larger than our benchmark shows: Semantic
Scholar alone indexes over 225M papers (Kinney et al., 2023), compared with 190k in our corpus.
What stands between current systems and both milestones is an expert-level sense of which ideas
matter, and we hope SCHOLARCATALYST offers a starting point for developing it. If undiscovered
public knowledge (Swanson, 1986) is as common as our results imply, models capable of expert
judgment across fields could unlock a large backlog of progress hidden in plain sight.

B.1
BENCHMARK INSTANCE
SCHOLARCATALYST evaluates whether a retrieval system can find prior work whose ideas would
have been insightful for pursuing a research project, rather than papers that are merely topically sim-
ilar. Each instance is anchored in a completed source paper and pairs its central research question
and distinct research directions with author-validated positive papers, topically related hard nega-
tives, and a temporally restricted search corpus. The 207 author-validated instances contribute 894
queries: 207 core research queries and 687 subfield-specific queries. This appendix first defines the
information in one benchmark instance (Section B.1), then reports the composition of the instances
and corpus (Section B.2), and finally examines the kinds of inspiration relations the benchmark
captures (Section B.3).

B.3
QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS OF INSPIRATION RELATIONS
This section provides further detail on the analysis of author-identified inspiration in Section 4.3 and
presents additional qualitative examples. To examine the inspirations captured by SCHOLARCAT-
ALYST beyond topical relevance, we qualitatively analyze author-provided rationales for catalyst
papers. We first introduce a multi-label taxonomy of inspiration relations, and then present case
studies illustrating cross-subfield transfer, the context-dependent role of prior work, and the imper-
fect correspondence between citation and inspiration. The authors’ accounts offer a rare view of the
research process that finished papers seldom document: which prior ideas researchers found use-
ful, and why. We find that these judgments are diverse in kind and unrelated to similarity, and that
neither citations nor an LLM reading the finished paper can recover them.
Inspiration relations are diverse and not captured by similarity. Prompting an LLM judge to
classify each of the 663 key-inspiration rationales under a nine-type taxonomy, we find that authors
most often credit a technique they adapted (method), empirical findings that supported a direction
(evidence), an idea extended to a new setting (generalization), or a limitation that motivated a new
approach (limitation), with more than one type applying to 55.8% of rationales (Figure 7). Similarity
to the query does not identify these papers: measuring lexical similarity as the fraction of unique
query words in a candidate’s title and abstract, and semantic similarity as cosine similarity under
Qwen3-Embedding-8B (Zhang et al., 2025b), hard negatives are at least as similar to the query as
positives on both measures (Table 11). This also limits agentic search, where grep and embedding
tools construct the candidate pool before an agent can reason about which papers could inspire a
project.
Citation Status Is an Imperfect Proxy for Inspiration. Citation status and author-identified inspi-
ration can diverge in both directions. Table 12 illustrates one source-paper instance in which two of
four CoreQ positives are uncited, while two cited papers are hard negatives. The uncited positives
informed the source paper’s exploration and offline-learning mechanisms, whereas the cited hard
negatives are topically adjacent but did not shape its approach. This pattern also appears at scale:
43.6% of SubQ positives are not cited by the source paper, and 67.8% of those share no references
with it. Even among cited papers, citation intent does not single out inspirations: only 46.2% of
CoreQ and 34.0% of SubQ positives carry a uses or extends label (Lauscher et al., 2022).
Diversity of Inspiration Relations. Prior work inspires new research through diverse mechanisms,
including direct method transfer, generalization to a new setting, limitations of existing work, and
conceptual reframing. To characterize how a key inspiration paper shaped a target work, we define a
nine-type taxonomy, given in Table 13, and prompt an LLM judge to label each core-research-query
key-inspiration rationale with every type it clearly expresses. To capture the ways a single paper
can shape a project, we allow each rationale to receive multiple type labels. The rationales receive
1.69 labels on average, with 55.8% receiving two or more and 12.7% receiving three or more. This
pattern shows that inspiration often operates through several mechanisms at once.

EVALUATION SETUP FOR PRE-DISCOVERY RETRIEVAL
Our main setting is retrieval from a temporally filtered local corpus. For each query, we exclude
corpus papers published after its source paper, so that the retriever can access only literature available

size per query. Although the temporal cutoff removes later work, the searchable corpus remains
large, with a median of 190,574 papers across both query types.
C.3
AGENTIC SEARCH BASELINES
Shared setup.
Under the main setting, agents search a temporally filtered local corpus of paper
titles and abstracts, with no access to the web or the source paper, and their backbones have knowl-
edge cutoffs preceding all source papers. The corpus-search tool returns the ten papers the retriever
ranks highest under the temporal cutoff. All calls use temperature 0, except GPT-5.6 backbones
(Section D.3), which set a reasoning effort instead, and Claude Fable 5.1, which uses its default
thinking setting.
Agents return rankings of different lengths, so we score each on a depth-100 list. The list starts with
the agent’s ranking, adds up to 25 papers the agent saw but did not rank, in discovery order, and fills
the rest with the retriever ranking of the original query, without duplicates or the source paper. The
cap of 25 keeps an agent that sees many papers but ranks few from displacing the retriever back-fill.
R@5 and R@20 thus come from the agent’s own ranking unless it holds fewer than k papers, which
happens mainly for the grep agent (440 of 894 queries at R@20, against 10 and 95 for the tool-
calling and deep research agents, and at most 93 for any Claude Fable 5.1 row). Trajectory recall is
the share of gold papers among the papers an agent sees during its run.
Tool-calling agent.
The agent combines the ReAct function-calling pattern (Yao et al., 2022), the
five-round budget of PaperScout (Pan et al., 2026), and the search-then-select design of PaSa (He
et al., 2025). It first searches the query as written, then runs up to 5 rounds, each one backbone reply
with any number of search calls. Returned papers enter a pool of at most 60. The backbone then
scores each pooled paper from 0 to 10 from its title and abstract, by how much its ideas could help a
researcher pursue the query, and the scores sorted with ties in discovery order give the ranking. We
do not run PaSa or PaperScout as released, because both search the web and PaperScout releases no
20

checkpoint of its trained policy, whose base model has no vendor-stated knowledge cutoff. We omit
their citation-expansion step because the corpus has no citation graph resolved to corpus papers.
Deep research agent.
The agent follows the plan, search, read, and synthesize procedure of deep
research systems (OpenAI, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a), with the same search tool and o3 (OpenAI,
2026e) as backbone. It splits the query into at most 6 sub-questions, searches the query as written,
then makes up to 20 searches, 10 reads, and a pool of 150 papers. A read returns the paper’s title
and abstract. After every 5 calls, a reflection step asks which sub-questions remain uncovered. A
final synthesis call ranks up to 50 pooled papers by usefulness, and this list is the ranking, with no
per-paper judge.
Grep agent.
We re-implement DCI-Agent-Lite (Li et al., 2026), which searches the raw corpus
using shell commands rather than a retriever. The released harness has read and shell tools and
a 300-turn budget; ours has one shell tool, 20 rounds, and results truncated to 4,000 characters.
The corpus is given as monthly files with one paper per line (identifier, title, abstract, date), and
the prompt asks the agent to search abstracts with regular expressions and return an ordered list of
identifiers. The backbone is GPT-4.1.
Claude Fable 5.1 rows.
We also run all three agents with Claude Fable 5.1 (Anthropic, 2026),
which compares the procedures using a single backbone. Its June 2026 knowledge cutoff follows all
but 6 of the 207 source papers, so these rows fall outside the rule of Section 4.1 and are marked in
Table 3. The model’s safety classifiers sometimes decline a call, which we treat as an empty reply.
Declines occur on 47 of the 894 queries for the tool-calling agent, 95 for the deep research agent,
and 33 for the grep agent. About half of the deep research agent’s declines are labeled as requests for
the model’s reasoning, almost all at or right after the reflection step; all others are labeled biology
or cyber-security risk. A declined judge call ranks the paper after all scored papers, a declined loop
turn ends the search, and a declined synthesis leaves the deep research agent with only the seen
papers and the back-fill.
Analysis conditions.
The remaining conditions relax the main setting and are used only for anal-
ysis in Section D.3, not for direct comparison with the query-only agents. The last three give extra
information to the tool-calling agent with Gemini 3.7 Flash (Google DeepMind, 2026c), and all are
back-filled to depth 100 as above.
• Closed-book (recall-from-memory). A backbone may know the source papers’ bibliographies
from training, so part of its agent’s recall could come from memory. To measure this, the backbone
receives the query and the source paper’s title and abstract, has no tools, and names up to 30 prior
papers. Each title is resolved to the top hit of a retriever search under the temporal cutoff, with no
similarity threshold, and we drop duplicates, titles whose search returns nothing, and the source
paper.
• Source paper. This measures how much the query-only restriction costs, since a real user would
know more than the query. The agent’s first message includes the source paper’s title and abstract.
• Web search. This tests whether web access, where the source paper and its citations may appear,
can substitute for the local corpus. The agent also gets a web-search tool (ten results per call) and
a page-reading tool (pages truncated to 6,000 characters, at most five per query), and must call
a tool in its first two rounds. A web result enters the pool only if its address contains an arXiv
identifier or its normalized title matches a corpus paper. Other results are shown to the agent but
not pooled.
• Bibliography. This gives an upper bound on what any leak of the answer could achieve. We map
the source paper’s bibliography, taken from its arXiv HTML version, to corpus papers by arXiv
identifier or exact title (46 of 54 entries on average). The leak upper-bound row of Table 22 adds
these papers to the agent’s pool and prompt, and the bibliography-alone row ranks them in citation
order without a model.

D.1
EXTENDED RESULTS FOR EMBEDDING RETRIEVERS
This section reports extended results for a broader set of recent embedding retrievers, including
models trained on scientific documents and models optimized for reasoning-intensive retrieval. Re-
cently, several embedding retrievers have been developed for reasoning-intensive retrieval, where
identifying relevant documents for complex queries requires reasoning beyond surface-level seman-
tic matching (Xiao et al., 2024; Su et al., 2025; Tchuindjo et al., 2026). We evaluate SciNCL (Os-
tendorff et al., 2022), OpenScholar (Asai et al., 2024), GTE-Qwen2-7B (Li et al., 2023), Rea-
sonEmbed (Chen et al., 2025), INF-X-Retriever (Yao et al., 2025), Llama NV-Embed-Reasoning-
3B (NVIDIA, 2026), and DIVER (Long et al., 2025). Interestingly, none of the reasoning-optimized
models improves over the strongest general-purpose embeddings. Their lack of gains suggests that
current objectives for reasoning-intensive retrieval do not adequately capture the relationships that
make a prior paper useful for advancing a research project. As discussed in Section 5, closing this
gap may require an expert-level sense of which ideas matter, enabling retrieval of useful connections
across fields beyond the scope of any individual expert.

D.3
POST-CUTOFF BACKBONES AND BENCHMARK LEAKAGE
Setup. Our main experimental setup keeps every backbone’s knowledge cutoff before every source
paper and gives the agent neither the source paper nor web access. This section asks what happens
when these restrictions are lifted: whether newer backbones gain from having seen the source papers,
and how much the source paper, the web, or its bibliography would help. We use two slices of the
23

author-written set, matched by primary arXiv category. The pre-cutoff slice contains 23 source
papers published between February 12 and December 23, 2025, with two cs.SE slots filled by cs.CE
and cs.MA papers. The post-cutoff slice contains 23 source papers published between May 8 and
August 14, 2026, four months later. A backbone whose cutoff falls between the slices can have seen
the first but not the second.
The pre-cutoff slice is harder for every system (core research query retriever R@100 of 0.49 against
0.63), so we compare each system with the retriever within the same slice. Table 21 evaluates
five backbones and their closed-book counterparts, and Table 22 varies the availability of access to
source papers, the web, and the bibliography for Gemini 3.7 Flash.
The cutoff split shows no sign that stronger backbones gain from having seen the source pa-
pers. With GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Fable 5.1, the tool-calling agent exceeds the retriever at core
research query R@20 on both slices, while the other three backbones stay close to it (Table 21).
Because every system scores higher on the post-cutoff slice, we compare each backbone’s margin
over the retriever within a slice. For GPT-5.6 Sol, whose cutoff falls between the slices, the margin
is the same on both (0.07 and 0.06), so it shows no gain from having seen the earlier papers. Claude

not settle the question. At the subfield-specific query level, no agent or closed-book row exceeds the
retriever on either slice.
With the same source context, search adds more to weak backbones than to strong ones. Fig-

The source abstract helps, web search does not, and the bibliography nearly gives away the
answer. With Gemini 3.7 Flash, the source title and abstract raise core research query R@20 by

paper appears in the web results for 85 of the 203 queries. The bibliography alone contains nearly
all gold papers (R@100 above 0.9). The main protocol therefore withholds the source paper, its
bibliography, and the web.
D.4
QUERY GENERATION METHODS
These methods test whether rewriting the research question with an LLM brings retrieval closer to
the gold papers, which often use different vocabulary from the question. All use GPT 4.1, with the
prompts in Section G.2 as the system message and the research question as the user message.
• Single-query expansion. The model’s output, the question plus a short continuation, replaces the
question as the retrieval query.
• Multi-query generation. The model returns up to five augmented questions, each used as a
separate retrieval query. Their top-1,000 rankings are fused with reciprocal rank fusion (k=60,
Cormack et al. (2009)).
• HyDE (Gao et al., 2023). The model writes five hypothetical abstracts of prior papers, and each
is used as a retrieval query, with the rankings fused as above. Its user message is the question
prefixed with Research question:.

D.5
READING FULL PAPERS VERSUS ABSTRACTS
Agents in the main setting see only titles and abstracts. An abstract states a paper’s main result
but often omits other critical details, so a gold paper could look unrelated to the question from its
abstract alone. Indexing full text is not a simple fix, since embedding-based retrievers handle long
documents poorly (Zhu et al., 2024) and a paper’s body far exceeds the input length they are trained
on. We therefore keep search over abstracts and instead let the agent read more of the papers it finds,
testing whether that extra text helps it judge and pursue candidates.
Full-text subset.
Full text is available for 180,399 corpus papers, converted from arXiv HTML,
LATEX source, or PDF and split by section heading. A query qualifies when all of its gold papers have
full text. A source paper enters the subset when its CoreQ qualifies and at least one of its SubQ does,
giving 158 CoreQ and 403 SubQ. We sample 50 queries of each type, separate from the 100 queries
used in the other analyses. The search tool and the back-fill are restricted to the full-text papers.
Reads share the five-round budget with searches. All other settings follow that of the tool-calling
agent.
Read policies.
Four policies add to the abstract the introduction, the method sections, the bibli-
ography, or both the introduction and the bibliography. A fifth returns the full paper, and a sixth
lets the agent request one section from a list returned by a second tool. Introductions are matched
by their heading, methods by headings containing method, approach, model, framework, algorithm,
or architecture, and bibliographies by references or bibliography. If no heading matches, the tool
returns the abstract. Each read is capped at 12,000 characters, about 41% of the body at the median
for papers read in full, and an included bibliography has its own 6,000-character cap.
D.6
ANALYSIS OF AGENT SEARCH FAILURES AND RATIONALE EXPLANATIONS
We analyze why agentic search fails to improve over retrieval, studying three possible causes in turn:
• Coverage. We measure how many gold papers agents find outside the retriever’s candidates.
• Ranking. We trace three search trajectories to see where gold papers are found and lost.
• Recognition. We check whether agents explain the gold papers they rank highly the same way
the authors do.
Coverage: agents rarely find gold papers beyond the retriever’s pool. Table 26 splits gold papers
by two outcomes, whether Gemini-Emb-2 retrieves them in its top 100 and whether each agent ranks
them in its own top 20. The retriever’s top 100 covers 70% of gold papers, and the agents recover
almost none of the rest. About half of gold papers are returned by no search during an agent’s run.
The first search, which uses the query as written, accounts for about two-thirds of the gold papers an
agent sees, and follow-up searches add few. The grep agent, which writes its own search commands
instead of using the retriever, keeps only 8% of the gold papers the retriever covers.
Ranking: agents drop many of the gold papers they do find. Among the gold papers in the
retriever’s top 100, the tool-calling and deep research agents each keep 57% and 48% in their top

Recognition: agents usually explain gold papers the way the authors do. For the gold papers an
agent does rank highly, we check whether its backbone understands why they matter. For each of
the 238 core research query gold papers in the tool-calling agent’s top 20, GPT-4.1 explains from the

this explanation with the author’s rationale. Of the 219 pairs whose author rationale is long enough
to compare, 76% describe the same relationship, and almost all others describe a different but valid
one (Table 30). Because the same model writes and judges, this agreement is an upper estimate. The
analysis also shows only that the backbone can explain a paper’s relevance once given it, not that
this understanding is why the paper entered the ranking.

AUTOMATED DATA CONSTRUCTION PIPELINE
We construct SCHOLARCATALYST through an end-to-end pipeline that transforms raw arXiv papers
into benchmark instances for inspiration literature retrieval. Starting from recent computer science
papers, the pipeline constructs a collection of source papers, reconstructs benchmark annotations,
assembles a retrieval corpus with a time cutoff, and augments benchmark instances through candi-

Existing publicly available scientific corpus (Lo et al., 2020) and kaggle arXiv datasets6 do not

paper into a structured representation with explicit citation links that supports all subsequent stages
of benchmark construction.

E.3
CANDIDATE RETRIEVAL
We augment the benchmark beyond the paper’s cited bibliography by recovering insightful papers
that were not cited. For each retrieval query, we retrieve the top-15 candidate papers from the corpus
using both sparse (BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009)) and dense (Qwen3 8B embeddings (Zhang
et al., 2025b)) retrieval systems, deduplicate them, and rerank with a reasoning model (Gemini-
3.6-flash) that scores each candidate for scientific relevance to the query rather than mere topical
similarity. We retain the top 10 for author review, where authors mark inspiring papers as positives
and closely related but non-inspiring papers as hard negatives.
Retrieval corpus. We construct a retrieval corpus containing 190,896 papers. The corpus includes
resolved references cited by our source papers together with a pool of 181K additional arXiv papers
published between 2020 and 2024, drawn from five main computer science domains (cs.CL, cs.CV,
cs.LG, cs.RO, and cs.AI), each restricted to papers with at least 1 citation. To construct a realis-
tic literature pool beyond the cited literature, we estimate the citation distribution of recent ICML,
ICLR, and NeurIPS oral and highlight papers and use it to determine the publication-year and cate-
gory distributions of additional corpus papers. At retrieval time, we additionally apply a per-query
temporal cutoff based on the query’s source-paper publication date, restricting candidate documents
to those published no later than the query paper itself. This precludes temporal leakage, ensuring
that no candidate postdates the query paper it is retrieved for.

ANNOTATION INTERFACE
We developed a web-based interface through which authors annotate their own research projects.
For each source paper, authors write or revise a research question intended to reflect their pre-
solution information state. They organize the question into distinct research subfields and identify
prior papers that provided meaningful scientific inspiration for each direction. Authors then review
additional candidate papers, labeling papers that provided meaningful inspiration as positives and
plausible but non-inspirational papers as hard negatives. For each judgment, they provide a rationale

resulting author-written instances contain research questions, positive and hard-negative labels, and
open-ended rationales.

Hi Robot: Open-Ended Instruction Following with Hierarchical Vision-Language-Action Models
(cs.RO)
Core Research Query
Robots working in human-centric settings receive language that is far messier
than the atomic commands studied in language-conditioned imitation learning.
A line of work trains policies to map instructions such as ’pick up the coke
can’ to actions and generalizes well within that regime, but real requests carry
constraints, preferences, and mid-execution corrections ("that’s not trash",
"I’m allergic to pickles") whose meaning depends on what the robot is currently
seeing and doing. A separate line of work uses LLMs and VLMs off the shelf to
decompose long-horizon tasks over predefined or hand-designed skills. These
systems parse richer language but inherit the limited dexterity of their skill
libraries and rarely react to a user who interrupts mid-task. Both lines leave
a gap between the semantics a system can interpret and the physical behavior
it can actually produce, and it is not obvious whether that gap is best closed
by making language interpretation more capable or by making dexterous learned
policies more promptable. How can a robot with dexterous, learned manipulation
skills interpret open-ended prompts and real-time human feedback, grounded in
its own observations, and act on them while the task is underway?
Subfield-Specific Query 1
Vision-language-action models fine-tune pretrained VLMs to emit low-level
continuous actions conditioned on images and a language instruction, and have
shown strong dexterity and some language generalization on real hardware.
However, their instruction supervision comes from demonstration annotations that
are short and literal ("put the cup on the plate"), so it is unclear how much
of their language-following ability survives when a prompt carries constraints,
negations, preferences, or a correction issued partway through a rollout. What
language can an end-to-end VLA actually be steered by at execution time, and
where does its instruction-following break down as prompts depart from the
literal commands seen in its training data?
Subfield-Specific Query 2
A line of work uses pretrained LLMs and VLMs to reason over a scene and break
a long-horizon goal into a sequence of calls to predefined or hand-designed
skills. These models bring broad semantic and world knowledge, enough to infer
that a paper cup is trash while a reusable one is a dish, or that "something
sweet" means candy, but they have never seen the robot’s observations, its skill
repertoire, or what its execution actually looks like mid-episode. What limits
an off-the-shelf foundation model’s ability to serve as a robot’s high-level
reasoner: can it stay grounded in the robot’s own visual stream and affordances
across a long rollout, and does its output remain something the low-level
controller can physically carry out?
Subfield-Specific Query 3
A line of work already lets users correct a robot while it acts. Some approaches
edit trajectories from verbal feedback, others train recovery behaviors from
simulator-constructed perturbations, and others route spoken corrections
through a language channel between a high-level and a low-level policy. Each
demonstrates that mid-execution language can redirect behavior, but each is
bounded: by a single task prompt, by the specific correction phrases human
annotators happened to produce, or by feedback that never depends on what
the robot is currently seeing. Utterances like "that’s not trash" or "leave
it alone" are unresolvable from text alone. They refer to whatever is in the
gripper right now. What determines the range of corrections such a system can
absorb, and can a robot ground feedback in its live observations while also
handling phrasings and situations that never appeared in its interaction data?

Source Paper Towards Understanding the Mechanisms of Classifier-Free Guidance (cs.CV) (NeurIPS
2025 Spotlight)
Core Research Question
Classifier-free guidance (CFG) is a highly effective inference-time guidance
method that significantly improves sample quality and condition alignment
in diffusion models, but its underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood.
Existing theoretical analyses mainly study isotropic Gaussian mixtures or
one-dimensional distributions, where much of the complex structure of natural
images is absent. This made us wonder (i) whether we could construct a
simplified model in which the effects of CFG on image structures become directly
visible, hence capturing the underlying mechanism of CFG more faithfully than
the simplified settings considered in prior theoretical analyses and (ii)
whether the mechanisms identified in this simplified setting extend beyond
itself and can help explain, at least partially explain the CFG’s mechanism in
real-world diffusion models?
Key Inspiration Paper: Abid, Zhang, Bagaria & Zou (2018). Exploring patterns enriched in a dataset
with contrastive principal component analysis. Nature Communications 9, 2134.
Rationale
Contribution:
This paper shows that the eigendirections associated with
differences between the covariance structures of two datasets can reveal
features that are distinctive to one dataset relative to the other. These
directions, referred to as contrastive principal components, characterize
prominent structures that distinguish the target data from the background data.
New
idea
I
brought
into
my
own
research:
When analyzing CFG in our simplified
linear model, I found that the guidance term involves the difference between
two covariance matrices: the covariance of the conditional distribution and
that of the unconditional distribution (CPC guidance component in equation
(12) of my paper). This suggested that the effect of CFG might be understood
through the structure of this covariance difference. I then searched for prior
work studying such differences between covariance structures and came across
contrastive PCA. This connection suggested that the relevant eigendirections
could identify structures that are particularly prominent in the conditional
distribution relative to the unconditional distribution, which we later show
contribute to the CFG’s effect.
Why I expect that idea to work:
The mathematical structure arising in our analysis
of CFG closely resembles that underlying contrastive PCA: both are governed
by differences between the covariance structures of two distributions. This
similarity suggested that the interpretation of contrastive principal components
could provide useful insight into how CFG emphasizes structures that distinguish
the conditional distribution from the unconditional one.
